# Common Ground — Design Reflection

Common Ground imagines the internet in 2086 as a shared digital garden where memories, ideas, and acts of care can grow. My design is meant to empower people whose contributions are often overlooked online: quiet participants, newcomers, older neighbors, and anyone who wants to share something without competing for attention. A passing thought can become a seed, and returning with another detail can turn it into a living story.

This matters to me because I want online spaces to make ordinary experiences feel worth keeping. A small memory, a useful skill, or an invitation to sit together can help people recognize something of themselves in others. For a community, these exchanges can preserve knowledge and create relationships across generations. The garden imagines a welcoming third place where participation can happen slowly, without popularity scores or pressure to post every day.

Peer feedback helped me see that the original atmosphere was stronger than its explanation. Farhan asked for more engaging storytelling, while Julianna suggested that memories could keep developing. Zhee Loc wanted more meaningful gardening, and Yixuan and Ziying responded to the flowers and their visual potential. My professor’s feedback made clarity a priority. I therefore added a three-step introduction, writing prompts, a guided example, and a “My seeds” filter. Visitors can now add chapters to their own stories: one addition produces a bud, and another produces a flower.

The dark background creates a quiet, futuristic setting. Lavender, pink, and mint flowers make different contributions feel vivid and welcoming; layered petals and leaves make growth visible. Clear buttons explain actions directly. Consistent navigation connects the vision, the interactive garden, and the community agreement, while CSS Flexbox adapts the layout to smaller screens.

My responsibility as a designer extends beyond visual appeal. Sound is optional, motion can be reduced, and controls support keyboard use. The prototype labels fictional stories and saves personal writing only in the browser. A real shared garden would require consent, accessible community research, and accountable moderation. I want this design to invite connection while being honest about those responsibilities.
